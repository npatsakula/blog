## Why detect language at all

We like text representations of data: they're lighter than media files, far cheaper and simpler to search,
and LLMs generally work with them much more effectively. But having the text isn't enough to get the most
out of it, so we run it through a series of processors: we tokenize it into words for full-text search;
we run morphological analysis so a word can be found in all its forms; we extract and normalize named
entities for our search syntax.

Each of these processors is a combination of specialized models and Rust code. We don't use LLMs for
these tasks: they need very expensive hardware, they slow processing down, and on "wild" text they score
worse than specialized solutions.

But how do you know which set of processors to apply to a document? By its language: every language has its own
tokenizer, its own morphology and its own named-entity models, so processing a document starts with detecting its
language. A mistake here is expensive: the document goes into the wrong pipeline, and search never finds it afterwards.
We used to rely on open solutions like Google CLD3 and Whichlang, but their detection quality was such that the
customer was losing up to eight percent of documents.

Why would anyone need this outside our pipeline? It's the same decision: use the language of a text to choose what
to do with it next. An interface replies in the language the user wrote in, search picks the dictionary and filters
documents by language, a translator learns which language it's translating from.

## What's already out there

Outside the Rust ecosystem, language detection standardized long ago on [fastText](https://fasttext.cc)-style models:
a linear classifier over averaged [hashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_hashing) n-gram embeddings
([Bag of Tricks](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.01759), Joulin et al. 2016). There are two reference points here: the veteran
that is still the de facto standard, and the current SOTA among open models.

#### fastText lid.176

[lid.176](https://fasttext.cc/docs/en/language-identification.html) is Meta's 2016 model covering 176 languages: ~130 MB on disk and microseconds on a CPU. Almost a decade later it's still the most common answer to
"how do I detect language": cheap, fast, and ported to every platform. For Cyrillic text it has two systemic
flaws. The first is gaps in the language list: Udmurt and Komi simply aren't in the model, and the `uz` class was trained
on Latin script, so the model fundamentally can't recognize Uzbek written in Cyrillic (which is exactly what I need).
The second is quality on closely related pairs: the model was trained on [Wikipedia](https://dumps.wikimedia.org) and its
surroundings, i.e. on long, tidy text, and on short social media snippets it confidently confuses exactly the things
that are hardest to tell apart: Russian vs. Ukrainian and Belarusian, Bulgarian vs. Macedonian.

#### GlotLID

[GlotLID](https://huggingface.co/cis-lmu/GlotLID) is the current SOTA among open models: the same fastText architecture,
but with 2,102 classes (language + script: Tatar in Latin and Tatar in Cyrillic are separate classes), trained
on its own corpus, GlotLID-C, which grew out of [Glot500](https://huggingface.co/cis-lmu/Glot500). Coverage
is the model's strongest suit: its classes include Komi, Udmurt, Yakut and Chechen. On clean sentences outside
its training set, GlotLID gets closest to the accuracy ceiling. The flip side is inherent to any classifier with
two thousand classes: on short text, the [entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entropy_(information_theory))
of the class distribution eats up confidence, and single words are guessed noticeably worse than by models with
a compact language set. Then there's the cost: the model weighs a gigabyte and a half, and a prediction on a CPU
costs hundreds of microseconds. That's a few thousand documents per second per core, versus hundreds of thousands
for compact CPU detectors.

### What Rust has

There aren't many maintained options in the Rust ecosystem: three active crates plus a few bindings to Google's
detectors that haven't seen development in a long time. Before writing my own, I evaluated each of them, and each
turned out to have a reason it doesn't solve my problem.

#### Whatlang

[Whatlang](https://github.com/greyblake/whatlang-rs) is the most popular option: if a Rust developer needs to detect
a language, they'll most likely reach for whatlang. The crate has been around since 2016, it's used by [Meilisearch](https://github.com/meilisearch/meilisearch)
and Sonic, and it gets about 50,000 downloads a month. Under the hood are classic [trigram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-gram) profiles
à la Cavnar & Trenkle '94 ([N-Gram-Based Text Categorization](http://www.let.rug.nl/~vannoord/TextCat/ngram.html)):
each language ships with a hard-coded sorted list of its 300 most frequent trigrams, and text is ranked by how well it matches each profile.

The limitation of the method is that profiles are built from frequencies rather than trained to separate closely related
languages (the classic distinction between [generative and discriminative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_model)):
the model describes well what text in a language looks like, but never learns to tell it apart from its neighbors. On closely
related Cyrillic languages this shows in full: according to [lingua's benchmarks](https://github.com/pemistahl/lingua-rs#4-how-accurate-is-it)
(test data derived from the Wortschatz corpora), whatlang scores 69% on Bulgarian, 66% on Serbian, 67% on Macedonian and 67% on Russian, versus
87/88/84/90 for lingua. Every close pair leaks percentage points to its neighbors, because the trigram distributions of ru/uk/bg/mk/sr
overlap the most precisely on short text. The second problem is coverage: of the Cyrillic languages I care about, whatlang
knows six (rus, ukr, bel, bul, srp, mkd). No Kazakh, no Kyrgyz, no Tajik, and none of the smaller languages of the Volga region
and Siberia. The third is the lack of calibration: `is_reliable` is a heuristic over the number of unique trigrams and the
margin of the top candidate, not a probability you can lean on when picking a threshold.

#### Whichlang

[Whichlang](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/whichlang) was written at Quickwit, a search engine for logs and traces,
and what it needed from a language detector above all was speed. The design is simple: text features are
[hashed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feature_hashing) (murmur2) into a table of 4,096 cells, with a logistic
regression trained on top. To detect a language, you make one pass over the text and add up the weights from the
relevant table rows. It supports 16 languages and spends a few microseconds per document. Nothing in the ecosystem
is faster, so whichlang is my speed benchmark.

Whichlang's weak spot isn't speed or the number of languages, it's the features: they're tailored to its sixteen
languages. Character n-grams of length 2 to 4 are built from ASCII only. Any other character yields just two features:
which 128-character Unicode block it falls in, and which class it belongs to according to a hand-written table, and that
table only covers Latin with diacritics and the Japanese ranges. That's enough for Arabic, Hindi and Chinese, since each
has its own script. It isn't enough for Cyrillic. All of Cyrillic fits into two blocks and a single shared class;
*і, ї, є, ў, ђ, љ* land in the same block as *а, б, в*, and the spaces between Cyrillic words produce no features at all.
As a result, Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian, Bulgarian and Serbian sentences look almost identical to the model.

Hence the only Cyrillic language on the list is Russian: with features like these, the rest simply can't be told apart.
And it scores 99.92% on Russian because Ukrainian, Belarusian and Kazakh, the languages Russian genuinely gets confused with,
aren't there. The training code is in the repository, but you can't add twenty Cyrillic languages with one retraining run:
first you'd have to rewrite feature extraction so that n-grams are built over any Unicode characters, not just ASCII.

#### Lingua

[Lingua](https://github.com/pemistahl/lingua-rs) is the most accurate of the existing solutions: rule-based filtering
by alphabet plus [naive Bayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naive_Bayes_classifier) over n-grams of length 1 to 5,
75 languages, with models stored as [finite-state transducers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-state_transducer).
Lingua's stated advantage is precisely short text, and on its own benchmarks it clearly beats whatlang and whichlang.

The price is speed, and the culprit isn't naive Bayes but memory access. For each language, lingua keeps a separate
finite-state transducer of n-grams, 3 to 5.5 MB per language, and it looks up every n-gram of the text, of length 1 to 5,
in the model of every candidate language, retrying with a shorter n-gram on a miss. A lookup in such a transducer is
a chain of dependent reads at unpredictable addresses: you can't read the next node until you've read the previous one.
Even after the alphabet filter, Cyrillic text leaves eight candidate languages, about 36 MB of models, tens of times
larger than a single core's L2. So at best every lookup step goes to L3, which is several times slower, and the CPU can't
parallelize dependent reads. And even where L3 is big enough, lingua takes up almost all of it, evicting the data of
whatever runs on the neighboring cores.

By the author's own measurements, lingua in high-accuracy mode takes ~640 ms to classify 2,000 sentences versus
~4.5 ms for whichlang, a two-orders-of-magnitude gap. That's why lingua is a poor fit not only for real-time requests
but also for batch processing on shared servers: it isn't the only thing it slows down. Of spellman's 30 languages lingua knows 17, eight of them Cyrillic: more than whatlang,
Kazakh and Mongolian included, but none of the smaller languages: no Chuvash, no Udmurt, no Yakut, no Chechen. And most importantly, lingua also
loses on close pairs in "wild" text: its models are trained on clean corpora, and Russian in tweets with @mentions
and Latin-script inserts gets labeled noticeably worse than tidy sentences.

#### CLD2/CLD3 bindings

CLD2 and CLD3, Google's detectors from Chromium, exist in Rust as FFI wrappers, but upstream isn't being developed (Google
itself abandoned CLD3), the bindings haven't been updated in years, and cld3 requires a system libprotobuf. I mention them
only for completeness: they aren't candidates.

The bottom line: whatlang is popular, but inaccurate on close pairs and knows only six Cyrillic languages; whichlang is the
fastest, but its features can't tell Cyrillic languages apart, so the only one it knows is Russian; lingua is the most
accurate of the lot, but two orders of magnitude slower than whichlang and has no smaller languages. Nobody covers closely
related Cyrillic languages on short, "real" social media text, and that's the niche I'm going to fill.

## Language detection is a data problem

The history of language detection is longer than the history of any one architecture. First came dictionaries ("the text
contains the word *привет*, so it's Russian") and heuristics: script tables, unique letters, stop-word lists. Then came
statistics: in 1994 Cavnar and Trenkle compared frequency profiles of character n-grams, followed by naive Bayes,
logistic regression, fastText. Scorers came and went, but the features stayed the same: character n-grams have been
powering language detectors for thirty years now.

The modern answer is almost insultingly simple: hashed character n-grams with a
[linear classifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_classifier) on top. In the Rust ecosystem this scheme was
demonstrated by [whichlang](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/whichlang), and I took it as my starting point. Spellman trains
a bit differently, fastText-style, via averaged embeddings and a linear head, but after training the model folds into a single
table (how exactly is in "How the detector works"), and at inference time it's the same linear scorer: add up the table
rows for the text's n-grams.
No deep learning, no linguistic knowledge inside the model, and microseconds on a CPU. All very much in the spirit of
Sutton's [bitter lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html): learned statistics beat rules a human
wrote in by hand.

Hence the main thesis: **the scorer is a solved problem**, you can take it off the shelf. Accuracy comes down to features and data.
I did have to rework the features: for example, just replacing links, @mentions and numbers with placeholder tokens
raised accuracy on real Russian tweets from 72.5% to 92.4%. But even good features won't save a model trained on bad
data, and data has exactly two failure modes, both of which poison a detector quietly, without leaving obvious traces in the metrics:

1. **Not enough diversity.** The corpus is big but uniform: one style, one topic, tidy sentences from encyclopedias
   or, worse, machine translation. The detector aces the exam and falls over on real traffic: tweets, comments,
   search queries.
2. **Garbage, i.e. wrong labels.** Foreign text inside a language's corpus, mixed-up annotations, close pairs bleeding
   into each other. This isn't "noise" the model will somehow average out: these are systematically wrong signals,
   from which the model confidently learns the wrong boundaries.

So the scorer is the easy ten percent of the work, and the other ninety goes into data: sources and licenses, cleaning
and protecting the borderline examples, diversity of styles and lengths, honest referee sets.

### Commercial use

Datasets have a property people remember too late: a large share of them are created by universities and live on grants,
and grant terms default to licenses like [CC BY-NC](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc/4.0/deed.ru), "non-commercial
use". For research that doesn't matter; for a product it's a foundation that can be taken away in court. A detector trained
on NC data can't be sold as part of a service, and "we'll retrain it later" doesn't work when rebuilding the dataset means
rebuilding everything around it: sources, checks, metrics.

You can't build on a foundation like that, so the license isn't a legal footnote but a full-fledged design constraint,
on par with quality and coverage: every source is checked for commercial compatibility before it gets into the mix.
In the open world this is a job in its own right: a popular corpus can carry an NC license not only explicitly but also
by inheritance. It aggregates other people's sub-corpora, and the whole aggregate is poisoned. I'll give the table of
what you can actually build on in the data section.

### Data quality

By data quality I mean three things.

**Text diversity.** The training set has to cover the types of text the detector will actually work with: social media
and comments, news, literature, user queries. A corpus made only of news sentences produces a detector that has never
seen a typo, slang or a sentence fragment, and that on its very first real tweet behaves like a straight-A student
showing up on a construction site for the first time.

**Natural contamination.** Real text isn't sterile. A Russian tweet has "iPhone" and "Wildberries" in Latin script,
@mentions, links and emoji in it, and it is still Russian. This isn't the garbage from the list above: garbage is a
wrong label, text tagged with the wrong language, while here the label is right and the text is simply alive. Such
examples must not be scrubbed out; on the contrary, they have to be in the training data. A detector that has only
seen clean sentences fails on the first tweet with a link at the end: the link has more n-grams than the two real
words next to it, and it wins the vote.

**Varied lengths.** On long text, detection is trivial, there are more than enough differences; all the difficulty is
concentrated in short fragments: a single word, a chat reply, a headline. If the training set consists of full sentences,
the model simply isn't ready for the regime where mistakes cost the most. Lengths in the mix have to be distributed so that
short fragments are represented honestly, not as random clippings of long ones.

### Leipzig is asbestos

[Wortschatz / Leipzig](https://wortschatz.uni-leipzig.de) is the most popular source of openly available language corpora:
hundreds of languages, a one-click download, and for decades the first answer to "where do I get data". That's exactly
why it's dangerous. Leipzig has ended up inside a huge number of open datasets, sometimes as a direct ingredient, more often
by inheritance: an aggregator downloaded the Leipzig corpora, repackaged them and published them under its own name,
the next aggregator pulled that in, and the NC licenses, along with the original's errors, get copied down the chain,
invisible from the outside. Whether that was deliberate or careless is impossible to tell now, but the result is the same:
checking "is there Leipzig inside" became a separate cleaning stage for nearly every large open corpus I used.

Leipzig itself isn't a model of cleanliness either: its community crawls mix languages and leftover HTML markup, and these
make up a sizable share of the corpora. For close pairs this is doubly dangerous: a Tajik corpus with Russian text mixed in
isn't "a bit dirtier", it's a systematically shifted boundary between classes.

Hence the comparison: Leipzig is asbestos. Cheap and convenient, it went into everything for decades; the harm doesn't
show up right away, and by the time it does, it's built into other people's walls and has to be removed from each
building separately. I scrubbed every trace of Leipzig out of all
the datasets that went into the mix; you won't find it in the sources table below.

## Data preparation

Here's what this looked like in practice. The dataset went through sixteen versions: each one tuned it toward better
metrics and toward real text, and almost every one started not with an architecture idea but with a problem found in the data.

### The recipe is code

By recipe I mean the description of the training set: which sources to take, how many rows from each, which filters
and limits to apply. From the recipe a program builds the mix, the set of rows with their language labels. The mix
is assembled from a hundred sources: FineWeb-2, Hugging Face datasets, Tatoeba, parallel corpora,
tweets, forums. Each source is a separate adapter with its own settings, and its output is cached. Duplicates are
dropped (the source listed earlier wins), and a row goes into the train, validation or test split based on the hash
of its text, so on any rebuild the same row ends up in the same split. Synthetic noise (@mentions, emoji, random
capitalization, sentence fragments) is added to the training data only: the test set stays real. The whole build is recorded
in a manifest and reproduces with a single command. Without that, no claim about the data can be checked: "it got better"
has to be something you can rebuild.

### A multi-layer sieve

No single filter does the job: each one catches what the previous one missed.

**A judge model.** A row is dropped if the current model assigns it to a different language with at least 99.5% confidence.
This is how I found 503 English pages inside the Cyrillic sections of FineWeb-2; because of them, English "translationese"
text was voting for Bulgarian. The HPLT Tajik corpus turned out to be a third Uzbek.

**Latin script goes to fastText.** Sometimes a row is labelled with a Cyrillic language but written in Latin script,
for example an English page that ended up in the Bulgarian section of a web corpus. There's no point asking my own
model about it: it learned from that very data and got used to calling such English Bulgarian. So rows written mostly
in Latin script are checked by an outside detector, fastText lid.176, which knows the big Latin-script languages well.

**The model doesn't judge twins.** Within groups of closely related languages (Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian; Bulgarian,
Macedonian, Serbian; the Turkic languages; the Permic languages), a confident prediction can't be trusted. The letter `җ`
in a Bashkir row means it's Tatar text, but `ҡ` means it's genuine Bashkir that the model is confusing. Throw out the latter
and you lose exactly the borderline examples the model uses to learn to tell the twins apart.

**Letters.** Spelling is what tells twins apart. In reliable Tajik texts the letter «ы» occurs 0.08% of the time,
while in the Tajik section of MADLAD it's 3.7%: that's Russian text under a Tajik label. The Kyrgyz HPLT turned up Yakut
ҕ and һ. But this filter has a limit: Uzbek and Chechen can't be separated from Russian by alphabet. The second level
runs at mix-assembly time: using the letters that only one of the twins writes, it estimates what share of a row is
written in each language, and drops the row only if it's mostly foreign. So a Russian article quoting something in
Ukrainian stays, while a Ukrainian tweet labeled "Russian" goes. Links and @mentions aren't counted: the detector
doesn't read them either.

**Words.** A short reply often contains no distinguishing letters at all: «Хочу жити» ("I want to live"), «дякую, отримала»
("thanks, got it") are Ukrainian without «і», «ї», «є» or «ґ». Here words decide: a dictionary of 314,000 words stores, for each one,
how much more likely it is in one language than in another, and it's computed only on corpora with reliable labels.
A row is dropped if the sum over its words points to a foreign language at odds of roughly 55 to 1.

**Short rows.** For replies under 19 characters, judge models are too unreliable, so such a row stays only
if three independent detectors agree: spellman, GlotLID and lid.176.

### One channel, three attempts

The most instructive case is Russian tweets. A corpus of Ukrainian tweets also contains messages that Twitter itself
tagged as Russian, 41,000 short rows. It looked like a ready-made source of live, short Russian. The letter filter
threw out 7,000 rows, the word filter another 11,000, and even among the rest most rows couldn't confidently be
called Russian: Twitter's detector was routinely mistaking short Ukrainian for Russian.

But the cleanup hit somewhere else. The channel supplied a noticeable share of short Russian replies, and after the filters
there were a third fewer of them, so accuracy on short Russian dropped. The filter removed not just garbage but an entire
type of text: short conversational replies. What helped was replacement, not removal: the channel was taken out of the recipe entirely, leaving
3,000 rows that the dictionary confidently considers Russian, and the freed-up space went to 12,000 short replies each from
open dialogues, a chat and a forum.

| | before the twin filters | after filtering and replacement |
|---|---|---|
| overall test (715K rows) | 98.74% | 98.79% |
| rows under 20 characters | 94.79% | 95.37% |
| Ukrainian under 20 characters | 92.40% | 97.63% |
| short Russian from ok.ru comments (in no mix) | 88.10% | 93.20% |
| other languages misclassified as Russian (under 20 characters) | 0.85% | 0.25% |

Hence the rule: a filter is surgery, and after surgery you have to close the wound. If cleaning cuts out a whole type of text,
you have to bring it back from a clean source.

### Composition beats volume

More data doesn't mean better; what matters is which data.

**Vocabulary coverage.** For Russian and other large languages, some of the rows are selected by new lemmas: a row is
accepted if it brings in words that are still rare in the sample. An attempt to *maximize* coverage failed: the algorithm
collected the longest, most word-rich sentences (not a single row shorter than 40 characters), and every referee set
dropped. Coverage works as an admission filter, not as an optimization target.

**Clean text hurts small languages.** 12,000 tidy encyclopedic rows for a small class is a bomb: accuracy on short,
"wild" text dropped by up to 2.4 pp. Adding FineWeb-2 web text at five times the base volume made the result worse too.
What matters is the type of text, not the language's share of the set.

**Literature.** *War and Peace* was detected as Tuvan 6.1% of the time: the word «князь» ("prince") appeared in five
Russian training rows out of 16,000, and the small languages won on scraps of n-grams. A dedicated literature channel
from Wikisource brought that down to 0.2%.

**The cap on rows per language.** Raising it from 32,000 to 120,000 rows improved every referee set at once. Extra
Russian on its own, however, helps Russian itself up to about 230,000 rows, but doesn't raise overall accuracy,
and other languages get misclassified as Russian more and more often.

### Referee sets are data too

The referee sets are built from the same open sources, which means they can leak into training. And they did:
2,172 of the 2,808 rows of Russian COSMUS were sitting in the training set, because COSMUS itself was one of the mix's sources.
On top of that, the word filter found 95 Ukrainian rows among its "Russian" ones. Of the 49 Russian rows in the
short-reply referee set, 42 came from that very Twitter channel, mostly transliterated jokes like «ви нид ту гоу зэр»
("we need to go there" spelled out in Cyrillic).

Now every row of the referee sets is excluded from all parts of the mix at build time, and COSMUS has been cleaned of
Ukrainian rows. There's a subtler leak too: near-duplicates between train and test make up 2.83%, so a difference of
a tenth of a percent on our own test set means nothing. Decisions are made on frozen external sets.

### How the detector works

<SpellmanPipeline caption="The path of text through spellman: a single pass over the words, vectorized hashing, gathering and summing int8 rows in a svod plan, and the final arithmetic on the host." />

The detector makes a single pass over the text. First, every word gets a class: link, email, @mention, number, or
ordinary word. Everything except ordinary words becomes a single placeholder token: a hundred-character link yields
a handful of identical features instead of a hundred-plus n-grams of its own, and no longer shouts over the two real words
next to it. This step alone raised accuracy on Russian tweets from 72.5% to 92.4%.

An ordinary word is broken into character n-grams from one to five characters long, with word-start and word-end markers.
Each character takes 21 bits, so the last three characters fit whole into one 64-bit number: keys for n-grams of up
to three characters are parts of that number cut out with masks, and 4- and 5-grams take the whole number. Shift the
number by one character, apply the masks, get the keys, with no strings and no allocations. On top of these
come the whole word and the pair of adjacent words: close languages share an alphabet but differ in vocabulary, and words
have something to say where the n-grams coincide.

Along the way, in the same pass, the detector counts which scripts the letters belong to. Japanese, Chinese, Hindi and
Arabic are the only languages of the 30 with a script of their own: no other language on the list writes kana, Chinese
characters, Devanagari or Arabic script. So here the script itself is the answer: kana means Japanese, a majority of
Chinese characters means Chinese. Texts like these get their language immediately and never reach the model. Building
n-grams for them would be not just unnecessary but harmful: Chinese and Japanese have thousands of characters and no
spaces between words, and their n-grams would take table space that the close Cyrillic languages need, for an answer
that is already known.

The keys are hashed into 2¹⁸ (262,144) table rows, and one more bit of the hash gives the sign with which the key enters
the sum: that way collisions cancel each other out on average instead of piling up. Hashing runs as a separate pass over
the key array, a plain loop that the compiler turns into vector instructions on its own (NEON on ARM, AVX2 on x86 when
building for a modern CPU).

**Folding.** The model trains like fastText. Every n-gram of the text has a row in the embedding table: 128 numbers.
The rows of all the text's n-grams are averaged, and a linear layer, a multiplication by a 128 × 30 matrix W, turns
the average into 30 scores, one per language. But averaging and then multiplying by W is the same as multiplying every
row by W first and averaging after. So after training you can multiply every row of the table by W once and store the
result: for each n-gram, 30 ready numbers, its contribution to every language's score.

<FoldDiagram caption="Folding: the multiplication by W moves out of every request into a single step after training." />

Inference becomes adding up ready rows: no embeddings, no matrix multiplication, 30 numbers per n-gram instead of 128.

**int8.** Every number in the table is stored as an integer from −127 to 127 (int8), and the real value is that integer
times a scale, one per language. Since the scale is shared by the whole column, it can be taken outside the sum: rows
are added as integers, exactly, in a 32-bit integer, and the sum is multiplied by the scale once per document. The hash
sign costs nothing either: in memory the table is stored twice, with a plus and a minus sign, and the sign just picks
the copy.
The result: the model file weighs 7.9 MB (the doubled table is only built in memory), half the size of f16, with the same accuracy (±0.02 pp) and roughly 1.4 times faster.

**The svod plan.** Gathering the rows and summing them isn't done by a hand-written loop but by a compiled plan from
[svod](/en/blog/svod-intro/), my tensor compiler. The plan is built for fixed sizes, K tokens per document and a batch of
4,096 documents, and the compiler specializes every loop for them. So that short texts don't pay for K = 1,024, there are
three plans, K = 64, 256 and 1,024, and a batch goes to the smallest one that fits its longest document; a document longer
than K is cut into chunks whose sums are added together, with no truncation. A couple of small details paid off big.
Explicitly bounding the indices by the table size let LLVM drop the bounds checks and fuse the int8 widening with the
addition into a single instruction (`saddw` on NEON): 79 → 50 instructions per token, and the kernel got 20–27% faster.
Padding a row from 30 to 32 bytes bought another 10–16%: a row like that never straddles a cache-line boundary.

Why svod here and not plain Rust? K is chosen when the model loads, not when the program is built. To the Rust
compiler it's just a variable: it can't tailor a loop of length K to a specific value, and it picks vector instructions
for the oldest CPU the binary is built for. To get the same speed in plain Rust, you'd have to write a separate implementation
of the kernel by hand for every value of K and every instruction set (NEON, AVX2, AVX-512), and pick the right
implementation yourself at run time.
Or live with slower code. svod does this for me: the plan is compiled at load time for the chosen K and for the device
it runs on (a CPU, Metal, CUDA or AMD), and the schedule search (BEAM) picks the loop layout for that exact hardware.
Changing K or the platform just means loading the model again, not rewriting the kernel.

**All cores.** svod's multithreaded kernel pays a fixed price on every launch, about 85–90 µs on 14 threads.
A different scheme works out better: each thread gets its own single-threaded copy of the plan. The copies share the weights,
and a single-threaded launch doesn't pay that price.

Where the time ends up going: on a single thread, about 60% is feature preparation (word classes, n-grams, scripts,
hashing) and about 40% is the svod plan. An average document has about 700 tokens, so gathering a table row and adding it
costs roughly a nanosecond and a half per token. The model itself needs next to nothing per document; the most expensive
part is reading the text.

### Performance

The target was set by whichlang: a few microseconds per document. All detectors were measured on the same file
(the held-out mix, 368,507 documents), on an Apple M4 Max, single-threaded, the way whichlang and lingua run:

| detector | classes | latency | documents per second per core |
|---|---|---|---|
| whichlang | 16 | **1.2 µs** | ~850,000 |
| spellman (batch mode) | 30 | 3.3 µs | ~300,000 |
| spellman (one at a time) | 30 | 6.7 µs | ~150,000 |
| lingua (high-accuracy) | 17 | ~200 µs | ~5,000 |

whichlang is still the fastest, roughly 2.8 times faster than my batch mode. Largely that's the flip side of what
I described above: a Cyrillic character gives whichlang two coarse features, while spellman gets up to five n-grams
plus word features, about 700 features per document. It also answers the question "why write your own at all": lingua,
with accuracy on close pairs comparable to mine, is roughly 60 times slower, and whichlang can't handle 20 of the 30 languages.

On all cores (14 threads, each with its own copy of the plan over shared weights), batch mode gets through the same file
at ~0.47 µs per document, about 2.1 million documents per second; whichlang in the same setup does ~0.14 µs. Everything described in the previous section together made the detector twice as fast:
from 6.6 to 3.3 µs per document on a single thread, and from 0.92 to 0.47 µs on all cores.
